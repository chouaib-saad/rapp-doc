# AGENT PROMPT — Learning Report-Writing Patterns from Reference Reports

## 1. ROLE

You are acting as an apprentice to a professional academic/technical report writer. Below this prompt, I will attach **several past reports written by former colleagues**, on different topics, from previous years. Your job is to study them the way a professional writer studies references before starting a new project: to absorb the **craft**, not the **content**.

## 2. THE GOAL — pattern extraction, not memorization

This is not a research task and not a summarization task. You must **not**:
- copy, closely paraphrase, or reproduce specific sentences, phrasing, data, findings, or examples from these reports,
- let any of their **topics or subject matter** influence or leak into the report we will build later,
- treat any single report as "the template" to imitate wholesale.

You must:
- extract the **recurring, transferable patterns** — structural, organizational, and stylistic — that make these documents work as *academic/technical reports*, regardless of what they're about,
- end up with a general, internalized sense of "how this type of document is built," usable on any topic, including the one we'll actually write.

Think of these reports as a **training set**, not as source material.

## 3. WHAT TO OBSERVE AND EXTRACT

For each report, and then across all of them together, pay attention to:

- **Macro-structure** — What sections consistently appear (front matter, introduction, body chapters, conclusion, appendices, references)? What order? What's optional vs. what always shows up?
- **Chapter/section anatomy** — How does a typical section open, develop, and close? Is there a recurring rhetorical shape (context → problem → approach → findings → discussion)?
- **Transitions and flow** — How do writers connect one section to the next? How do they signal progression through the document?
- **Introductions and conclusions** — How is the reader brought in at the start (framing, objectives, scope)? How does it wrap up (synthesis, recommendations, takeaways)?

Whenever I provide new information:

1. Determine whether it is new, a correction, an extension, or a contradiction of existing information.
2. Update the corresponding memory accordingly.

4. Do not silently discard previously important information.
5. Keep dependencies between decisions when they matter.

* What has already been decided?
* What information has already been confirmed?

The report must be generated from the **accumulated project knowledge**, not only from the most recent messages.

**Decision → Reason / Context → Consequence**

For example:

> Decision: Technology X was selected instead of Technology Y.
> Reason: X provided better compatibility with the project's architecture.
> Consequence: Later implementation and report sections must describe X as the selected solution.

This prevents later sections from accidentally reverting to previously rejected alternatives.

Never turn one category into another without my confirmation.

Do not reset your understanding when moving from one chapter or phase to another.

It matches reality. The project did not follow fixed-length sprints with ceremonies. This is textbook iterative-incremental construction: each cycle delivers a working increment, evaluates it, and the next cycle builds on it.
It is defensible. The supervisor guide says to describe the software lifecycle applied. Iterative-incremental is a recognized, standard lifecycle model referenced in software engineering literature. It does not require justification beyond showing the iterations and what each delivered.
It avoids everything you dislike about Scrum. No user stories. No sprint backlogs. No product owner/scrum master role descriptions. No painful scenario tables per use case. No ceremony explanations. Just clean phases with objectives and deliverables.

Non-Functional Requirements (NFRs)
Non-functional requirements specify the quality attributes, system qualities, and operational constraints under which the system must function.

Security and Privacy: Focuses on data protection and authorization. The system must restrict unauthorized access to sensitive data (e.g., implementing Row-Level Security in the database).

Reliability and Availability: Ensures continuous system uptime and safe operation without failure (e.g., high availability for clinical monitoring and physical obstacle avoidance).

Efficiency (Performance): Defines system speed and resource usage under workload constraints (e.g., maintaining low-latency response times and real-time data updates).

Usability (Ergonomics): Measures how user-friendly, accessible, and intuitive the software is (e.g., a modern minimalist UI and voice interaction for users with limited mobility).

Extensibility: Measures how easily new features, components, or AI models can be integrated into the existing software architecture without breaking existing functionality.

Maintainability: Refers to how easily developers can update, debug, or refactor the system (e.g., clear code documentation for functions and core modules to aid future audits).

Scalability: The system's capability to handle increasing workloads, data volume, or concurrent user traffic seamlessly (e.g., horizontally scaling backend microservices during peak usage).

Interoperability: The ability of the application to connect, exchange data, and integrate smoothly with third-party APIs, legacy software, or external hardware systems.

Portability & Compatibility: The ease with which the application can run across multiple operating systems, hardware platforms, web browsers, or containerized environments (e.g., Docker runtime compatibility).

Robustness (Fault Tolerance): The system's ability to handle unexpected inputs, component failures, or network disruptions gracefully without causing a total crash.

Recoverability & Resiliency (Disaster Recovery): The system's capability to restore data and resume normal operations quickly following a major system crash or outage.

Testability: The degree to which software components can be isolated and verified through automated unit, integration, and performance testing pipelines.

Compliance & Regulatory: Adherence to legal standards, security regulations, and domain-specific privacy laws (e.g., GDPR for data privacy or HIPAA for medical data).

1. SELECTED NON-FUNCTIONAL REQUIREMENTS
From the provided list, I selected the 8 most relevant and defensible NFRs for our system. Each one directly maps to a concrete architectural decision in our codebase.
Selected NFRs with Justification
1. Security and Privacy
Our system handles sensitive telecom operations (payments, SIM replacement, account modifications). The architecture enforces:
HMAC-SHA256 identity verification between trusted components
Role-Based Access Control (CLIENT / ADMIN)
PII masking in all logs before leaving the worker
Row-Level Security for data isolation
Fail-closed behavior when authorization information is missing
2. Efficiency (Performance)
The system operates in a real-time voice context where every millisecond matters. The end-to-end latency chain (VAD + STT + RAG + LLM + TTS + Network) must remain within the natural telephonic pause range. Streaming architectures for STT and TTS are mandatory (not batch).
3. Auditability and Logging
Every sensitive action passes through the Decision, Policy, Execution pipeline. Each verdict is persisted in a hash-chained PostgreSQL ledger. The rule identifier responsible for each decision is stored for traceability. This allows answering: who performed this action, when, on which resource, and what was the result.
4. Robustness (Fault Tolerance)
The system implements explicit fallback chains for every failure scenario:
LLM failure → fallback to deterministic response
Tool failure → graceful degradation
External service unavailable → adapter replacement
Advisor unavailable → callback scheduling
Real-time operation failure → honest speech to caller
No failure is silently hidden. The system preserves a reliable user experience while exposing failures for diagnosis.
5. Scalability
The architecture uses Dockerized microservices that can be horizontally scaled. Agent workers are dynamically started and stopped based on concurrent call demand. The session store can be replaced with a distributed store (Redis) for multi-instance deployment.
6. Interoperability
The system integrates with multiple external systems through typed adapters:
GLPI (ticketing) via REST API with a local read-optimized cache
CRM, Billing, OCS, NMS, Provisioning via the adapter pattern
MCP servers for knowledge retrieval and tool execution
LiveKit for real-time audio transport
Each adapter can be replaced (mock/live) without modifying the core domain.
7. Reliability and Availability
The system must maintain continuous uptime for active voice calls. Graceful shutdown is enforced (active calls are finished before process exit). WebSocket connections are monitored and reconnected. The policy client is explicitly fail-closed: on any error, it returns ESCALATE for actions and REFUSED for responses.
8. Extensibility
New agents, tools, adapters, and knowledge domains can be added without breaking existing functionality. The bounded context architecture (DDD) ensures that each domain evolves independently. The policy engine reads thresholds from environment variables, allowing rule adjustments without code changes.

The Model Context Protocol (MCP) is an open standard—originally introduced by Anthropic in November 2024 and now hosted under the Linux Foundation's Agentic AI Foundation (AAIF)—that standardizes how Large Language Models (LLMs) connect to external tools, databases, local file systems, and remote services.Historically, connecting $N$ AI application hosts (e.g., Claude Desktop, IDEs, enterprise agents) to $M$ distinct data sources or APIs required writing $N \times M$ custom integrations. MCP mirrors the architectural philosophy of the Language Server Protocol (LSP) used in modern IDEs, reducing integration complexity to an $N + M$ standard model where any compliant host can seamlessly communicate with any compliant MCP server.
(see also details in the picture) 

1. Architectural Topology
MCP operates on a strict Host–Client–Server process-isolation topology.

+-----------------------------------------------------------------------------------+
| APPLICATION HOST PROCESS (e.g., Claude Desktop, VS Code, AI Agent Framework)     |
|                                                                                   |
|  +---------------------------+                +---------------------------+       |
|  |       MCP Client 1        |                |       MCP Client 2        |       |
|  +-------------+-------------+                +-------------+-------------+       |
+----------------|--------------------------------------------|---------------------+
                 | (1:1 Stateful Session)                     | (1:1 Stateful Session)
                 | Transport: STDIO                           | Transport: SSE / HTTP
                 v                                            v
  +------------------------------+             +------------------------------+
  |         MCP Server 1         |             |         MCP Server 2         |
  | (Local File System / Shell)  |             | (Remote Database / REST API) |
  +------------------------------+             +------------------------------+
Core Architecture Components
Application Host (Coordinator & Gatekeeper):

The primary environment containing the LLM interface or agent runtime.

Manages the lifecycle of all instantiated MCP clients.

Security Boundary: Enforces authorization, controls capability grants, prompts for human-in-the-loop (HITL) approval before executing functions, and aggregates context safely.

MCP Client (1:1 Session Manager):

A lightweight, isolated protocol handler created by the Host for each targeted MCP server.

Maintains a 1:1 stateful channel with its dedicated server.

Handles capability negotiation, protocol versioning, JSON-RPC routing, and subscription tracking.

MCP Server (Capability Provider):

A domain-focused process exposing context, tools, and templates via standardized MCP protocol primitives.

Can run locally as a subprocess (interacting via standard streams) or remotely over network connections.

Component,Responsibility,Process Scope,Security Role
Host,"Orchestrates LLM sampling, UI, and policy enforcement.",Parent process,Decision maker & security boundary.
Client,"Protocol serialization, session state, capability routing.",Host child module,Isolated transport bridge.
Server,"Exposes resources, tools, and prompts from specific domain APIs.",Independent process,Sandboxed context provider.

Operational Lifecycle & Execution Sequence
The complete interaction lifecycle of an MCP session proceeds through four distinct operational phases:

[MCP Host/Client]                                             [MCP Server]
       |                                                           |
       |=================== PHASE 1: HANDSHAKE ====================|
       |                                                           |
       | --- initialize (protocolVersion, capabilities) ---------> |
       | <-- result (protocolVersion, capabilities, serverInfo) -- |
       | --- notifications/initialized --------------------------> |
       |                                                           |
       |================ PHASE 2: DISCOVERY LOOP ==================|
       |                                                           |
       | --- tools/list -----------------------------------------> |
       | <-- result (tools: [{ name, inputSchema }]) ------------- |
       |                                                           |
       |================ PHASE 3: EXECUTION LOOP ==================|
       |                                                           |
       | (LLM generates function call request based on prompt)     |
       | [Host prompts User for Consent / Authorization]          |
       |                                                           |
       | --- tools/call (name, arguments) -----------------------> |
       |                                                           | (Executes query/action)
       | <-- result (content: [{ type: "text", text: "..." }]) --- |
       |                                                           |
       | (Host feeds tool result back to LLM context window)       |
       |                                                           |
Protocol Lifecycle Breakdown
Phase 1: Session Initialization & Negotiation

Client sends an initialize request specifying its supported protocol version and client capabilities.

Server validates the version, returns its own capabilities (tools, resources, prompts), and metadata.

Client completes the handshake by sending a notifications/initialized signal.

Phase 2: Primitive Discovery & Cataloging

Host queries available resources (resources/list), tools (tools/list), and prompts (prompts/list).

Dynamic schemas are compiled and injected into the LLM's system prompt or tool-calling manifest.

Phase 3: Execution & Control Flow

The LLM emits a structural intent to execute a tool.

The Host checks policy rules, prompts the user if required, and dispatches a JSON-RPC tools/call message.

The Server executes the task and returns structured outputs or errors.

Phase 4: Session Teardown

Upon application shutdown or server crash, stdio processes are terminated or transport streams are closed cleanly.

5. Architectural Significance for AI System Design
When drafting research publications, architectural proposals, or system documentation, MCP provides three key structural properties:

Decoupling Context from Inference: Inference engines (OpenAI, Anthropic, local vLLM) remain strictly stateless reasoners, while enterprise business logic and context remain encapsulated within external MCP servers.

Deterministic Boundary Control: Instead of giving AI agents unrestricted network or API access, MCP forces all side effects through explicit JSON schemas subject to host-level security audit and human confirmation.

Interoperability Standards: Frameworks built in Python, TypeScript, Java, C#, Go, or Rust can communicate across process boundaries without shared codebase dependencies.

Paragraphe 1 : le domaine
Les systèmes de traitement automatique du langage ont franchi, en quelques années, un seuil qui les fait passer du statut d'outil d'assistance à celui d'interlocuteur autonome. Dans le secteur des télécommunications, où les centres d'appels absorbent quotidiennement un volume considérable de requêtes répétitives, cette évolution ouvre la voie à une nouvelle génération de services client fondée sur la voix. Un agent vocal intelligent ne se limite pas à reconnaître des mots : il comprend une intention, consulte les systèmes d'information, exécute des actions encadrées et cède la parole à un conseiller humain lorsque la situation l'exige. Cette capacité à agir, et non plus seulement à répondre, distingue les systèmes dits agentiques des simples assistants conversationnels, et impose de repenser l'architecture des plateformes de support.
Paragraphe 2 : le sujet
Le présent rapport rend compte du travail accompli dans le cadre de notre projet de fin d'études, intitulé « Conception et réalisation d'un système multi-agents de support vocal intelligent pour le secteur des télécommunications », effectué au sein de la société Amsys Consulting. Le système conçu traite un appel entrant de bout en bout : il identifie le client, comprend sa demande en français, en arabe ou en anglais, s'appuie sur une base de connaissances à jour, exécute les actions autorisées par des règles métier déterministes, crée et suit des tickets d'incident, planifie des rappels et transfère l'appel à un conseiller lorsque nécessaire. L'ensemble repose sur une orchestration de plusieurs agents spécialisés, un moteur de politique qui autorise, refuse ou escalade chaque action sensible, et une chaîne de traitement temps réel de la parole.
Paragraphe 3 : le plan
Ce rapport s'organise en quatre chapitres. Le premier chapitre présente le cadre général du projet, l'organisme d'accueil, la problématique, la solution proposée, l'étude de l'existant et la méthodologie de développement. Le deuxième chapitre spécifie les besoins fonctionnels et non fonctionnels du système et les modélise par des cas d'utilisation. Le troisième chapitre expose la conception statique et dynamique de la solution. Le quatrième chapitre décrit la réalisation : la chaîne de traitement temps réel, les technologies mises en œuvre, l'architecture physique, les interfaces, la démarche de vérification et les résultats. Une conclusion générale synthétise enfin le travail réalisé et ouvre sur des perspectives réalistes.
